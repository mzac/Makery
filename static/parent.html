Who uses this

One installation, one address, and a set of rules each. With only one person here nobody ever sees a sign-in screen; add a second and the app asks who is making things when it opens.

Off, each child sees only their own and cannot star, rename or trash anybody else's. It is a curtain rather than a lock: a direct link to a file still opens, because this page shows those pictures in plain image tags that cannot carry the PIN. For siblings on a home network that is the right weight.

Removing somebody deletes nothing. Their pictures stay on disk, stay in the nightly email, and stay visible here.

Open or closed

If the filter says no

Anything the word filter stops, or a photo the picture checker says no to, is refused and your child is shown a friendly message. This is what happens next. You are told either way — with the picture attached, if there was one.

The picture checker looked at an actual image and said no, which is a much stronger signal than a word on a list — the word filter also stops “gun” in a sentence about a water pistol. That is why the middle setting is the one worth having on.

Off means a photo or a drawing goes straight into their gallery. On, it is looked at first — and it still goes in if the checker cannot be reached, because it is their own camera roll.

Both off by default. That list is written for pictures, so it stops words a conversation or a song needs — “blood”, “gun”, “hanging” from a tree. A shorter list runs either way.

The relay password

Kept apart from everything else on this page, and worth saying why: the backup file holds every setting, and the backup is the file you email to yourself — a relay password does not belong in it. So this one is left out of the backup, never shown back to this page once saved, and never written into a log line or the activity log. Or one Apprise URL, used as it is

For a relay the boxes above cannot describe — Gmail with an app password, SendGrid, SES. Set one and it wins over every box on this card. It is only for this email; messages to your phone have their own box under “Messages to your phone”, and a URL in one of them never does the other one’s job.

The shape it exists for is mailtos://you:apppassword@gmail.com, so it is a password with a hostname in front of it — and it is treated like one: left out of the backup file, never shown back to this page once saved, never written into a log line. Messages to your phone

Telegram, WhatsApp, Signal, ntfy, Discord and about 150 others, through Apprise. One URL per service — for Telegram that is tgram://<bot token>/<chat id>.

These are Apprise URLs like the one on the Daily email card, and they are not the same box: these go to a phone, that one sends the nightly email. Put an mailto URL here and it gets every alert you have ticked below, not the summary.

Where they go

These URLs have a token in each of them, so they are treated like the relay password: left out of the backup file, never shown back to this page, never written into a log line.     What has happened

    Everything the app does that is worth writing down: what was made, what was deleted and put back, what the filter stopped, every setting change with its old and new value, who signed in, and wrong PINs.

    Nothing here deletes it. There is no button on this page and no route in the app that shortens the log. Each entry is sealed against the one before it, so an entry edited or removed afterwards shows up below as a break at a named position. To be plain about the limit: anybody with a shell on the server can delete the database. The app will not, and the chain says if somebody did.

    The hashes come with it, so a copy kept elsewhere can be checked against this one.

    How long to keep it

    Saved

    For ever is the default. Trimming writes its own entry saying it ran, so the gap it leaves at the front of the chain has an explanation sealed at the back of it. The words switch is off to begin with: which category stopped something answers "is the filter working", and the sentence itself is the child's.

    Backup and restore

    One file with everything you have decided: every setting, for the household and for each child, who the children are and what their faces look like, their characters, the words they have asked for before, the chat, the prompt overrides, the render timings and their banner. Not their pictures, videos or songs — those are the gigabytes, and they are already files on a disk you can copy. This one is a few hundred kilobytes and you can email it to yourself.

    Put one back

    Restoring replaces all of the above with what is in the file. It does not touch the gallery, and it never renumbers a child — their pictures stay theirs. A copy of what is here now is saved first, so this can be undone.

    Copies on the server

    One is made every night. Each kind is counted separately — the nightly ones, the copies saved just before a restore, and any you made by hand — so a run of nightly ones cannot push out the copy taken five minutes before you restored something.

    The PIN on this page

    It gates this page, the “I’m a grown-up” box on the warm-up sums, and adding a profile. Without one, anybody who can reach the app is a grown-up here — which is fine on a home network and not otherwise.

    Changed

    There is deliberately no button here that removes the PIN: turning the lock off from behind it is how a page like this is left open by accident. It is never shown back, never logged, and never put in the backup file. To take it off — or if it is forgotten — see “Forgetting the PIN” in the README; it needs a shell on the machine, which is the point.

    The helpers

    Three jobs, and which model in Ollama does each one. The list is what this machine has already pulled — this app never downloads a model. To add one, run ollama pull <name> where Ollama is (the names are in its library) and come back to this page.

    Saved

    Ollama and the picture maker share one graphics card, and a video render wants nearly all of it — so this and a render never sit on it together: the helper is unloaded before every render and loaded again when it is next asked something. A bigger model is not a risk to a render, then. It is a longer wait before the answer comes back, every time.

    How they are held

    How long Ollama keeps a model on the card after it has answered. 0 lets go of it at once and pays the load time on every tap; 60s or 5m trades memory back for speed. On this machine the card is shared with the picture maker, so 0 for the idea helper is the safe answer.

    Saved

    The helper’s name is a proper noun: it stays the same in all seven languages, and it shows on the Chat tab and in the line it opens with. Emptying the card before a render is what stops the two model sets meeting on it — turn it off only on a bigger card.

    How files are named

    What a new file is called, one pattern per kind of thing. A pattern is plain letters, numbers, dots, dashes and underscores, with {tokens} filled in as each file is made. The line under each box is what the next one would be called.

    Saved
    What you can put in a pattern

      Every pattern needs {n} or {time} in it, or two files would want the same name. {n} is counted here rather than by the picture maker, which counts by looking at the folder and hands a number straight back when something is deleted; this one only ever goes up.

      Nothing already in the gallery is renamed. A pattern applies to what is made from now on. Renaming what is there would break every thumbnail, every open tab and every note beside a file, and nobody would see a thing for it.

      How long things can be

      The two sliders on the child's page take their ends from here. Songs are cheap. Videos are not: memory on the graphics card goes with pixels times frames, and on this machine 15 seconds at the normal quality peaks near 15.5 GB of 16.3. Raising the video ceiling is the one number on this page that can make a render fail rather than just look different — move it a couple of seconds at a time and watch the VRAM figure on “Right now”.
